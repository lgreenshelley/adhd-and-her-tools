Built for kids in Year 4 to Year 8

High school asks more of an ADHD brain than primary school ever did: six classrooms, six teachers, deadlines in every direction, and a whole new sensory world. This screener looks at the skill areas that carry that load, explains each one in plain language, and shows you what is already going strong and where you could focus first.

The Readiness Snapshot

What do the bands mean?

Each skill area sits in one of three bands. Steady means it is an area of strength right now. Developing means it is coming along with support, which is exactly how skills grow. Focus area means this is where deliberate attention pays off next. Skills ebb and flow with age and load, so every band is a snapshot, not a final word.

A note before you read on

Progress since last time

Skills often wobble when load increases: a new school year, a new term, a big change. A dip straight after a transition usually reflects the bigger load, not lost skills. Watch the trend across two or three check-ins, not any single one.

Where to start

Why this order?

Because skills stack. It is hard for any child to use planners, checklists or study systems while their days still feel overwhelming, so we steady the everyday first, then build the organisational skills on top of that foundation. Nervous system regulation is the base everything else stands on. And because ADHD brains learn through movement, senses, rhythm, connection and play, every starting point above is built that way, not just worksheets. Occupational therapists describe this as working from the foundations up. It is also how I sequence my 1:1 work.

Who helps with what

If you decide to bring in more support, different professionals help with different parts of the picture. And do not overlook the school itself: teachers, year advisors and school counsellors are often the quickest first step for school-day supports. As a guide:

Your GP or paediatrician is the starting point for assessment, diagnosis, referrals and medication questions, and coordinates the wider team.

An occupational therapist works on the territory this screener maps: regulation, sensory processing, attention and executive skills, and everyday independence at home and school. That is my field.

A psychologist can help where anxiety, low mood or intense emotional reactions are a large part of the picture, supports friendships, social skills and rejection sensitivity, and conducts formal assessments, including of learning.

A speech pathologist helps where language, social communication, following verbal instructions, or reading, writing, comprehension and processing are the bigger challenge.

This is a map of who does what, not a referral.
